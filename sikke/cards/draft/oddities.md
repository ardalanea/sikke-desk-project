**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** On the island of Yap, large carved limestone wheels called fei (rai) served as money. American anthropologist William Henry Furness III recorded in his 1910 book that one family's wealth rested on a very large stone that had sunk in a storm while being towed home on a raft. The islanders agreed that its loss "a few hundred feet" offshore did not affect its value, and the family stayed rich. Ownership changed by common agreement, not by moving the stone. Milton Friedman retold the story in a 1991 Hoover Institution paper to argue that all money rests on shared belief.

**The surprising detail:** Nobody alive had seen the sunken stone, yet it still bought things.

**Echo today:** Bitcoin and bank balances also move ownership by shared ledger, not by moving anything physical.

**Uncertain points:** The date of the sinking is not given ("many years ago"). The Furness full text on archive.org was found but only Friedman's quotation of it was read in full; checker should confirm the passage in Furness directly.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** In Kievan Rus', marten skins (kuna) and squirrel skins (veksha) worked as money alongside silver. According to numismatist Dzmitry Huletski (KOINON, 2020), worn squirrel skins were bundled — typically 18 to a bundle — sealed with lead and treated as equal to one kuna, a system he dates to around the mid-11th century, possibly under Yaroslav the Wise. The Arab traveller Abu Hamid al-Gharnati, who visited Kiev in 1150, described "worn and torn squirrel furs fastened with a lead seal" circulating as money. Novgorod birch-bark letters of the 12th century use the same money terms.

**The surprising detail:** The skins were valued for the official seal, not the fur — so a ragged pelt still spent like new.

**Echo today:** It is the same idea as paper money: worthless material made valuable by an issuer's mark.

**Uncertain points:** The "18 skins per bundle" figure and the dating to Yaroslav are the author's interpretation; the paper was read via an academia.edu copy. A second numismatic source would help.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** The Wall Street Journal reported in October 2008 that pouches of mackerel ("macks") had become the main currency in much of the US federal prison system, after smoking was banned there around 2004 and cigarettes stopped working as money. A pouch was worth about $1. Inmates used macks to buy commissary goods, contraband such as prison-made alcohol, and services like shoe shines and cell cleaning. A supplier told the paper it had sold over $1 million of mackerel to federal prison commissaries the year before. The Bureau of Prisons said inmates caught bartering could be sent to isolation and lose good-behaviour credit.

**The surprising detail:** Few inmates actually eat the fish — it is valued because it is cheap, standard and keeps.

**Echo today:** In 2023 the WSJ reported Sam Bankman-Fried paid for a jail haircut in mackerel (lead only, not opened).

**Uncertain points:** The original WSJ article was not opened (paywall); details come from two secondary summaries. "Few eat it" is commonly reported but was not confirmed in an opened source — keep out of posts unless sourced.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** Since 1953, Italy's Credito Emiliano (Credem) has lent money to Parmigiano Reggiano makers against their cheese. Producers deposit wheels, each about 80 pounds, in two bank-owned warehouses run by a subsidiary, where the cheese matures, and the bank lends a typical 70–80% of their value. A 2015 Harvard Business School case put the warehouse capacity at 440,000 wheels. If a borrower defaults, the bank can sell the cheese. HBS reported Credem's degradation rate during aging at about 1%, against an industry average of 10%.

**The surprising detail:** The collateral gets more valuable while it sits in the vault.

**Echo today:** Euronews reported in August 2026 that extreme heat is straining Italy's "cheese banks" (lead only, not opened).

**Uncertain points:** Figures are from 2015 and may have changed. No aging period or per-wheel value was stated in the source opened.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** A line about compound interest being the greatest force or invention is widely credited to Albert Einstein. Quote Investigator found no credible evidence he said it. The earliest Einstein link it located was in the Wall Street Journal in August 1976, more than 20 years after his death, and even then hedged as "reported to have said". Similar praise for compound interest appeared much earlier without Einstein: Jack London in 1906, and a 1916 advertisement for the Security Investment Company that called it "the greatest invention the world has ever produced".

**The surprising detail:** The famous line most likely began as investment-company ad copy, not physics.

**Echo today:** The quote still opens countless finance-influencer videos and bank adverts.

**Uncertain points:** Absence of evidence is not proof; QI calls Einstein's authorship "very unlikely". Matches the known-myths list.
