**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** The earliest known coins date to the late 7th century BC in Lydia, whose capital Sardis lies near today's Salihli in Manisa province. Ancient authors (Xenophanes, Herodotus) credited the Lydians with inventing coinage. The first coins were electrum, a natural gold-silver alloy from the Pactolus river. Around the mid-6th century BC King Croesus replaced electrum with separate pure gold and pure silver coins. After Cyrus the Great took Sardis (mid-540s BC), the Persians kept minting Croesus's lion-and-bull coins for about 30 years, until Darius I introduced the gold daric and silver siglos around 515 BC.

**The surprising detail:** Persia's famous daric began as a re-design of a coin first minted in Anatolia.

**Echo today:** The phrase "rich as Croesus" still survives in English; Sardis is an open archaeological site in Manisa.

**Uncertain points:** Exact date of the very first coins is debated (late 7th century BC is the scholarly consensus cited); whether Lydia or nearby Ionian Greek cities struck first is not fully settled. Date of Darius's reform given as c. 515 BC in source.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** Paper money, called čāv (from Chinese chao), first circulated in Tabriz on 19 Shawwal 693 / 12 September 1294 under the Ilkhanid ruler Gaykhatu, copying the Mongol notes used in China. Per Encyclopaedia Iranica, the notes carried a Chinese inscription and the shahada, and the death penalty was prescribed for anyone who refused them. Prices rose more than tenfold, many residents fled, and trade in the bazaars came to a standstill. After a period of riot and confusion, the edict was rescinded.

**The surprising detail:** Iran tried paper money more than 350 years before Europe's first central-bank banknotes, and the Tabriz bazaar effectively vetoed it.

**Echo today:** A reminder that money works only when people trust it, not when it is ordered.

**Uncertain points:** Duration differs: Iranica describes about two months of riot and confusion; Tehran Times says Tabriz was deserted "in 3 days" and the decree was cancelled soon after. Note design details (mulberry-bark paper, denominations) come only from the Tehran Times piece. The "350 years before Europe" comparison is the reporter's framing (Stockholms Banco notes, 1661) and needs a source check.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** According to the Central Bank of the Republic of Türkiye, the first Ottoman paper money was issued in 1840 under Sultan Abdülmecid as "Kaime-i Nakdiye-i Mutebere" ("paper that counts as cash"). The notes were not printed: each was made by hand and stamped with an official seal. They worked more like interest-bearing debt notes or treasury bonds than modern currency. Because handmade notes were easy to forge, printing began in 1842 and the handwritten notes were exchanged for printed ones.

**The surprising detail:** An empire's first banknote was produced by hand, one sheet at a time.

**Echo today:** The word "kaime" survived in Turkish as a slang/old word for paper money.

**Uncertain points:** TCMB gives 1840 and the 1842 switch to printing; denominations and issue size of the first handwritten batch were not in the source opened. The "kaime survives as a word" echo is reporter's note, unsourced.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** In 1889 Baron Julius de Reuter obtained from Naser al-Din Shah the concession to found the Imperial Bank of Persia (Bank-e Shahi), including the exclusive right to issue banknotes in Persia. Iran's first official banknotes were therefore issued by this British-registered bank, denominated in tomans and krans. Per Encyclopaedia Iranica, the newly founded Bank Melli Iran took over note issue in 1930, paying £200,000 to Bank-e Shahi for the right. Before modern banks, merchants used a signed bill called bijak, and money dealers (sarraf) lent at 24–36% a year.

**The surprising detail:** The rial's ancestors were printed for a foreign private bank, and Iran had to buy back the right to print its own money.

**Echo today:** "Sarraf" is still the everyday Farsi and Turkish word for money changer.

**Uncertain points:** "Over 40 years" is reporter's arithmetic from 1889/1890 to 1930; the exact first-note date varies (1888 cashier's checks of the New Oriental Bank, Imperial Bank notes from c. 1890). The concession's stated duration (often cited as 60 years) was not confirmed in sources opened. "Sarraf still used in Turkish" (sarraf) is reporter's knowledge, unsourced.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** Cyprus's first coins were silver pieces struck by its kings in the late 6th century BC, dated by hoards found on the island and abroad. Evelthon of Salamis is the first king of Salamis whose name appears on coins; his sigloi show a recumbent ram and carry his name in the local Cypriot syllabic script. Legends on Cypriot coins appear in that syllabary or in Phoenician, and from the 4th century BC also in Greek. Seven kingdoms are confirmed as mints: Salamis, Kition, Amathous, Paphos, Marion, Soloi and Lapethos.

**The surprising detail:** The island's first coins spoke a script almost no one can read today, the Cypriot syllabary.

**Echo today:** Salamis ruins lie just north of Famagusta; Soloi and Lapethos are also on the island's north coast, so local readers can visit the mint cities.

**Uncertain points:** "2,500 years ago" is rounded reporter framing. Possible additional mints are uncertain per source. Echo uses geography only; keep neutral naming in the post (avoid political place-name choices).
