**Fact:** On 14 September 2007, after BBC business editor Robert Peston reported Northern Rock had sought emergency Bank of England funding, ordinary savers formed queues around British branches — the first run on a UK bank since 1866. Roughly £1 billion was withdrawn that first day alone. On 17 September, Chancellor Alistair Darling guaranteed all deposits to stop the panic; by then an estimated £2 billion had left the bank. Northern Rock was nationalised on 22 February 2008.

**The surprising detail:** Savers already had deposit insurance (100% of the first £2,000, 90% of the next £33,000) — academics still debate why insured depositors panicked anyway.

**Echo today:** The 2023 Silicon Valley Bank run showed the same pattern — digital queues this time, same underlying fear.

**Fact:** IT engineer James Howells mined 8,000 bitcoin in 2009 and kept the private key on a hard drive. In 2013, it was thrown out during a clear-out and ended up at Newport City Council's landfill in Wales. As the coins' value climbed into the hundreds of millions, Howells sued the council for access to dig it up or for compensation. In January 2025, the High Court dismissed the claim, citing Section 14(6)(c) of the Control of Pollution Act 1974, which makes anything deposited at a council landfill the property of the council. His appeal was refused in March 2025.

**The surprising detail:** A 50-year-old waste-disposal law, never written with digital money in mind, was the rule that decided who owns a fortune in bitcoin.

**Echo today:** Howells says he will take the case to the European Court of Human Rights — unresolved as of this research.

**Fact:** At 8:15pm on 8 November 2016, Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced that India's ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes — about 86% of all cash in circulation by value — would stop being legal tender at midnight. **Fact:** On 1 December 2001, as depositors stampeded to pull money out of Argentine banks, Economy Minister Domingo Cavallo froze nearly all bank accounts — the "corralito." Peso withdrawals were capped at 250 pesos a week; dollar accounts were frozen outright unless the holder agreed to convert to pesos. The government later forcibly converted dollar deposits to pesos at an unfavourable rate as the peso collapsed; the measure dragged on until mid-2003.

**The surprising detail:** Depositors who had saved in what they thought was a safe foreign currency woke up to find the government had converted their savings into a currency that then lost roughly three-quarters of its value.

**Echo today:** "Corralito" has since become regional shorthand across Latin America for any sudden government freeze on bank withdrawals.

**Uncertain points:** The $250/weekly figure is well corroborated across secondary sources but not pinned to a primary Argentine decree or IMF/BIS document directly.

**Fact:** Switzerland's 1934 Federal Banking Act made client secrecy a criminal offence for bankers to breach — originally meant to protect depositors, including European Jews who moved money to Switzerland in the 1930s for safekeeping. After the Holocaust, many depositors were dead and heirs couldn't produce account numbers. A US class action (1995), Senate hearings (1996), and a 1997 published list of dormant accounts eventually forced the issue. **Fact:** Between 6 and 9 October 2008, Iceland's three largest banks — Glitnir, Landsbanki and Kaupthing — all collapsed into receivership within days of each other. Their combined assets were more than eleven times the country's GDP. To stop the króna's collapse, Iceland imposed capital controls in November 2008 that weren't fully lifted until March 2017. **Fact:** Between 1892 and 1894, lending backed by stocks and bonds at the Ottoman Bank — then the empire's de facto central bank — nearly tripled, fuelled partly by speculation in South African gold-mine shares promoted by the bank's own governor. When share prices fell in London, the Galata bourse in Istanbul crashed, and by late October 1895 the Ottoman Bank faced a wave of withdrawals and banknote redemptions. It survived only "in extremis," propped up by Ottoman government support and the shipment of more than a million liras in gold from London.

**The surprising detail:** The bank that effectively managed Ottoman state finances was nearly destroyed by a stock bubble its own chief had helped inflate.

**Echo today:** A direct ancestor of the Northern Rock story (LCK-0001) above.
