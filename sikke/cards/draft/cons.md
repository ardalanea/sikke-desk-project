**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** In 1924–25 Artur Alves dos Reis used forged contracts to persuade Waterlow & Sons, the London firm that legally printed Portugal's banknotes, to print 500-escudo notes supposedly for the colony of Angola, with secrecy demanded "for political reasons". Waterlow used the genuine plates, so the notes were technically real. The fraud surfaced in December 1925 when notes seized in a raid on Reis's own bank in Porto included two with identical serial numbers. Reis was sentenced to 20 years in 1930. The Bank of Portugal later won £610,392 in damages from Waterlow in the House of Lords.

**The surprising detail:** The "forged" notes were genuine, printed by the official printer from the official plates; only the duplicate serial numbers exposed them.

**Echo today:** Authority-impersonation fraud: fake paperwork plus a "keep it secret" request still fools legitimate institutions.

**Uncertain points:** Volume differs by source (Damn Interesting: 580,000 notes, 290M escudos face value; PMG: over 100M escudos entered circulation). Exact damages figure and House of Lords year should be confirmed in the law report (Banco de Portugal v Waterlow & Sons, 1932). Reis's death date per Damn Interesting (8 July 1955); confirm.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** James Addison Reavis forged a chain of Spanish colonial documents, planted in archives, to claim a royal land grant covering about 12 million acres of central Arizona and western New Mexico. Railroads, mining companies and settlers paid him for title or rights; KJZZ puts the total above $5 million. Government investigators then examined the papers themselves: the ink, steel-pen strokes, type and paper were wrong for the 1700s, including paper watermarked by a Wisconsin mill. The Court of Private Land Claims rejected the grant in 1895, and Reavis was convicted of fraud, serving two years and paying a $5,000 fine.

**The surprising detail:** A "Spanish royal" document was printed on paper from a Wisconsin mill.

**Echo today:** Document forensics still catches forgeries — metadata is today's watermark.

**Uncertain points:** "$5 million" mixes cash and promissory notes (Wikipedia); actual cash received unclear. Conviction year given as 1895 or 1896 depending on source. Acreage expressed differently (12M acres vs 18,600 sq mi).

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** Thérèse Humbert claimed an American millionaire, "Robert Henry Crawford", had left her a 100-million-franc fortune, locked in a safe pending a lawsuit with his supposed nephews — a lawsuit she herself kept running. For about two decades Paris lenders advanced her huge sums against the unopened safe; estimates of the borrowing range from 20 to 250 million francs. When the safe was finally opened on 9 May 1902 it held no securities (accounts mention old newspapers, a button, a coin). The Humberts fled, were arrested in Madrid in December 1902, and Thérèse received five years' hard labour.

**The surprising detail:** Crawford never existed — the "heirs" suing her were her own invention, keeping the safe legally shut.

**Echo today:** "Locked funds" or "inheritance pending release" pitches remain a classic advance-fee story.

**Uncertain points:** Borrowed sums vary widely; safe contents differ by account; her death year is disputed (1918 Chicago vs alive in Paris 1930). Sentence and trial year (1903) to confirm against a French source (Gallica).

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** In the early 1820s Gregor MacGregor, a veteran of South America's independence wars, styled himself "Cazique of Poyais", a supposed Central American state, and floated a £200,000 Poyais bond on the London market. He also sold land and printed Poyais dollars, which some emigrants swapped their savings for. About 250 settlers sailed in 1822–23 and found jungle and no country as promised; about two-thirds eventually died. MacGregor was tried for fraud and conspiracy in France (December 1825, per History.com) but acquitted, was never convicted of any crime, and died in Venezuela in 1845 on a military pension.

**The surprising detail:** Investors bought government bonds of a nation that existed only in a guidebook and on a printed map.

**Echo today:** Glossy whitepapers for tokens of projects that do not exist.

**Uncertain points:** Clavel's research argues Poyais was a real (failed) settlement project on land granted by an Indigenous leader, not pure invention — the "fake country" framing is disputed. Bond size, settler numbers and deaths are from History.com; confirm in Clavel or a bond-market history. Trial date (History.com says December 1825; release eight months later) needs confirming.
