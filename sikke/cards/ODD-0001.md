**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** On the island of Yap, large carved limestone wheels called fei (rai) served as money. In his 1910 book The Island of Stone Money, American explorer and ethnographer William Henry Furness III wrote that a Yapese friend told him of a nearby family whose wealth was an enormous fei lying at the bottom of the sea. Generations earlier it had been lost in a storm while being towed home on a raft. Everyone agreed that "a few hundred feet of water off shore" did not affect its value. Big stones often changed owners without being moved. Milton Friedman retold the story in a 1991 Hoover Institution paper.

**The surprising detail:** No one, not even the family, had ever seen the stone, yet everyone accepted it as their wealth.

**Echo today:** Bank balances and crypto ledgers also move ownership by shared record, not by moving anything physical.

**Uncertain points:** The sinking is undated ("many years ago"; "two or three generations"). The story is second-hand: Furness heard it from his friend Fatumak; it is not an observed event. Friedman used it to argue that money rests on shared belief; some economists dispute reading Yap stones as "fiat money" (e.g. Moneyness blog, 2013) — present Friedman's point as his argument.
