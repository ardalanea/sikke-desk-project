**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** Paper money, called čāv (from Chinese chao), first circulated in Tabriz on 19 Shawwal 693 / 12 September 1294 under the Ilkhanid ruler Gaykhatu, copying the Mongol notes used in China. Per Encyclopaedia Iranica, the notes carried a Chinese inscription and the shahada, and the death penalty was prescribed for anyone who refused them. Prices rose more than tenfold, many residents fled, and trade in the bazaars came to a standstill. After a period of riot and confusion, the edict was rescinded.

**The surprising detail:** Iran tried paper money more than 350 years before Europe's first central-bank banknotes, and the Tabriz bazaar effectively vetoed it.

**Echo today:** A reminder that money works only when people trust it, not when it is ordered.

**Uncertain points:** Duration differs: Iranica describes about two months of riot and confusion; Tehran Times says Tabriz was deserted "in 3 days" and the decree was cancelled soon after. Note design details (mulberry-bark paper, denominations) come only from the Tehran Times piece. The "350 years before Europe" comparison is the reporter's framing (Stockholms Banco notes, 1661) and needs a source check.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** In 1924–25 Artur Alves dos Reis used forged contracts to persuade Waterlow & Sons, the London firm that legally printed Portugal's banknotes, to print 500-escudo notes supposedly for the colony of Angola, with secrecy demanded "for political reasons". Waterlow used the genuine plates, so the notes were technically real. The fraud surfaced in December 1925 when notes seized in a raid on Reis's own bank in Porto included two with identical serial numbers. Reis was sentenced to 20 years in 1930. The Bank of Portugal later won £610,392 in damages from Waterlow in the House of Lords.

**The surprising detail:** The "forged" notes were genuine, printed by the official printer from the official plates; only the duplicate serial numbers exposed them.

**Echo today:** Authority-impersonation fraud: fake paperwork plus a "keep it secret" request still fools legitimate institutions.

**Uncertain points:** Volume differs by source (Damn Interesting: 580,000 notes, 290M escudos face value; PMG: over 100M escudos entered circulation). Exact damages figure and House of Lords year should be confirmed in the law report (Banco de Portugal v Waterlow & Sons, 1932). Reis's death date per Damn Interesting (8 July 1955); confirm.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** On the island of Yap, large carved limestone wheels called fei (rai) served as money. American anthropologist William Henry Furness III recorded in his 1910 book that one family's wealth rested on a very large stone that had sunk in a storm while being towed home on a raft. The islanders agreed that its loss "a few hundred feet" offshore did not affect its value, and the family stayed rich. Ownership changed by common agreement, not by moving the stone. Milton Friedman retold the story in a 1991 Hoover Institution paper to argue that all money rests on shared belief.

**The surprising detail:** Nobody alive had seen the sunken stone, yet it still bought things.

**Echo today:** Bitcoin and bank balances also move ownership by shared ledger, not by moving anything physical.

**Uncertain points:** The date of the sinking is not given ("many years ago"). The Furness full text on archive.org was found but only Friedman's quotation of it was read in full; checker should confirm the passage in Furness directly.
