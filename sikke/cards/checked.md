**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** Paper money, called čāv (from Chinese chao), was first issued in Tabriz on 19 Shawwal 693 / 12 September 1294 under the Ilkhanid ruler Gaykhatu, modelled on the paper money of Mongol-ruled (Yuan) China. The notes carried Chinese characters and the Muslim profession of faith. An edict made refusing the notes, or failing to hand in coins for exchange, punishable by death. Prices rose more than tenfold, many people left the city, and trade in the bazaars stopped. After about two months of disruption the government first allowed coins for buying food, then withdrew the paper money altogether.

**The surprising detail:** Iran tried state paper money more than 360 years before Stockholms Banco issued what are usually called Europe's first banknotes (1661), and the Tabriz bazaar effectively forced the ruler to back down.

**Echo today:** A reminder that money works only when people trust it, not because it is ordered.

**Uncertain points:** Exact day: Rashid al-Din gives 12 Sep 1294 (Julian); Wassaf gives Dhu'l-qa'da 693 (Sep–Oct 1294). Duration "about two months" comes from the continuator of Bar Hebraeus (via Iranica) and Howorth; Howorth says the order was obeyed for about eight days before the shops emptied. No source found of anyone actually executed for refusing the notes (Howorth mentions executions of people involved in the disturbances). Note design details (mulberry-bark paper) come only from Tehran Times: do not use. Denominations "half a dirham to ten dinars" are confirmed by Howorth.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** In 1924–25 Artur Alves dos Reis used forged contracts, presented as a secret Bank of Portugal loan deal for the colony of Angola, to get Waterlow & Sons, the London firm that printed Portugal's banknotes, to print 580,000 notes of 500 escudos. Waterlow used the Bank's own plates (or plates from the same die), so the notes matched genuine ones. In December 1925, after a tip-off, police searched Reis's Banco Angola e Metrópole in Porto, and Bank of Portugal officials found serial numbers that appeared twice. Reis was sentenced to 20 years in 1930. In 1932 the House of Lords ordered Waterlow to pay the Bank £610,392.

**The surprising detail:** The notes came from the official printer and the official plates; they were exposed only when officials sorted notes by serial number and found duplicates.

**Echo today:** Authority-impersonation fraud: forged paperwork plus a "this must stay secret" request still fools legitimate institutions.

**Uncertain points:** Reis's exact death date differs (9 July 1955 per Banco de Portugal archive title and Wikipedia; 8 July per Damn Interesting): use "July 1955" or "1955". Sentence month differs (May vs June 1930): use "1930". The "for political reasons" quote is not confirmed; LSE says secrecy was justified by a claimed split among the Bank's directors. Hennies's conviction in absentia is not confirmed by two sources; leave him out.

**Fact (40–120 words, plain, no hype):** On the island of Yap, large carved limestone wheels called fei (rai) served as money. In his 1910 book The Island of Stone Money, American explorer and ethnographer William Henry Furness III wrote that a Yapese friend told him of a nearby family whose wealth was an enormous fei lying at the bottom of the sea. Generations earlier it had been lost in a storm while being towed home on a raft. Everyone agreed that "a few hundred feet of water off shore" did not affect its value. Big stones often changed owners without being moved. Milton Friedman retold the story in a 1991 Hoover Institution paper.

**The surprising detail:** No one, not even the family, had ever seen the stone, yet everyone accepted it as their wealth.

**Echo today:** Bank balances and crypto ledgers also move ownership by shared record, not by moving anything physical.

**Uncertain points:** The sinking is undated ("many years ago"; "two or three generations"). The story is second-hand: Furness heard it from his friend Fatumak; it is not an observed event. Friedman used it to argue that money rests on shared belief; some economists dispute reading Yap stones as "fiat money" (e.g. Moneyness blog, 2013) — present Friedman's point as his argument.
