# SK-2026-10-05 · Tabriz 1294: the paper money the bazaar refused — ENGLISH

Lead card: REG-0002 (Verified 2026-10-01). Series label: From Our Region.

## Telegram (EN)

**In 1294, a ruler in Tabriz made refusing his new money punishable by death. The money was made of paper.**

Gaykhatu, the Mongol ruler of Iran, had a vizier with an idea borrowed from China: print it.

On 12 September 1294 the new notes, called čāv, went into circulation in Tabriz. They carried Chinese characters and the Muslim declaration of faith on the same paper. Coins had to be handed in for exchange. The penalty for refusing the notes was death.

Tabriz obeyed for about a week.

Then the shops emptied. Prices in paper rose more than tenfold. Many people left the city, and the bazaar went quiet.

About two months later, the government gave in and withdrew the paper money.

The bazaar had won. Money works when people trust it, not because a ruler orders it. Europe's first banknotes came more than 360 years later, in Stockholm in 1661.

Would you have taken the paper?

Sources: Encyclopaedia Iranica · Howorth, History of the Mongols (1888)

## LinkedIn (EN)

In 1294, a government made it punishable by death to refuse its new currency.

The currency still failed within about two months.

The place was Tabriz, under the Mongol Ilkhanate. The ruler's vizier copied the paper money of Yuan China. The notes, called čāv, carried Chinese characters and the Muslim declaration of faith. Coins had to be handed in for exchange.

The city obeyed for about a week. Then the shops emptied, prices in paper rose more than tenfold, and the bazaar stopped trading. The order was withdrawn.

Three lessons that still hold:

1. Trust can't be decreed. A mandate can force a launch; only confidence keeps a currency, a product or a platform alive.
2. Markets vote with their feet. The bazaar didn't argue. It simply stopped trading.
3. Rules don't replace credibility. A death penalty could force people to hold the notes, but not to sell their goods for them.

Europe's first banknotes appeared more than 360 years later, in Stockholm in 1661.

Where do you see organisations today asking for trust before they have earned it?
