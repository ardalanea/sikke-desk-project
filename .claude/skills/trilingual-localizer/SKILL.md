# Trilingual Localizer

Adapt, don't translate word for word. A Farsi or Turkish reader should feel the post was written for them.

## Write native, don't translate

Translating the English post sentence by sentence is the single biggest cause of a post sounding foreign, even when every word is correct. Work this way instead:

1. Read the English post once, for the facts and the shape only (hook → build → payoff, or slide-by-slide beats). Note the fixed facts you must keep (see below).
2. Close the English version. Write the post in Farsi or Turkish from those facts, in sentences you would actually say — not sentences shaped like the English ones with the words swapped out.
3. Compare back to the English only to check facts and length. If a Farsi/Turkish sentence happens to mirror the English sentence's structure, that's a signal to rewrite it, not a sign you did it faithfully.

A native reader (or listener, for a reel) should not be able to tell an English draft existed. If you can "hear" the English sentence under the Farsi or Turkish one, it needs another pass.

### Tells that a line still reads as translated

**Farsi:**
- Long compound sentences carried over from English instead of breaking into the shorter sentences natural Farsi prose actually uses.
- Heavy reliance on «است» copula constructions and passive voice (translated-news register) where a native post would use an active, spoken-adjacent verb.
- Calqued connectors — «در واقع» for "actually", «به طور قابل توجهی» for "significantly" — where a native writer would just say «واقعاً» / «خیلی» or restructure the sentence instead of translating the connector.
- English subordinate-clause order kept intact ("Despite X, Y happened" → stiff «علی‌رغم X، Y رخ داد») instead of the order a Farsi sentence would naturally take, or simply splitting into two sentences.
- Reaching for formal Arabic-origin vocabulary where an everyday Persian word says the same thing (already a glossary rule — this is the same instinct applied sentence by sentence, not just word by word).

**Turkish:**
- English word order and clause structure kept under the Turkish words — Turkish is left-branching; a relative clause translated in English order almost always sounds foreign. Rebuild the sentence in real Turkish order instead of translating clause-by-clause.
- Subject pronouns ("O", "Bu") kept in every sentence the way English keeps "it"/"this" — Turkish drops them naturally; keeping them is one of the clearest translation tells.
- Idioms translated literally instead of swapped for the Turkish expression that actually carries the same feeling (or dropped if none fits).
- Formal written-register constructions ("idi" endings, bureaucratic nominalizations) in what should read like a lively social post.

## Fixed (never change)

Facts, figures, dates, names, legal status, sources, the order of claims, and the claim strength ("one of the first" stays "one of the first").

## Free to change

Hook wording, idioms, jokes, sentence rhythm, which detail goes first in a caption, local comparisons (a price in Türkiye or Iran readers know), hashtags, and the series label translation.

## Farsi (فارسی)

- Audience: Iranians in Iran, Cyprus, Türkiye and the diaspora. Modern written Persian, warm and clear; avoid heavy Arabic-origin formal words when a common word exists.
- Use correct half-spaces (ZWNJ): می‌شود، کتاب‌ها، بانک‌ها.
- Digits: Persian digits (۰۱۲۳۴۵۶۷۸۹) in body text. Thousands separator "٬" or plain spacing; be consistent.
- Dates: Gregorian date, and for events since 1925 add the Solar Hijri year in brackets, e.g. ۲۰۱۳ (۱۳۹۱–۱۳۹۲).
- Add «میلادی» after a Gregorian year on its first mention in a post, so readers don't take it as a Solar Hijri year. Medieval dates: give the lunar Hijri date with «ق» (e.g. ۱۹ شوال ۶۹۳ ق); do not invent a Solar Hijri equivalent.
- Hashtags: always include #Sikke #TEAMedia alongside Farsi tags (#سکه #تاریخ_پول).
- Iranian money: say whether a figure is in rials or tomans (1 toman = 10 rials).
- Punctuation: Persian comma «،» and question mark «؟», guillemets «» for quotes.
- Right-to-left: keep English names and tickers out of the start of a line where possible, to avoid RTL display glitches.

## Turkish (Türkçe)

- Audience: Türkiye and North Cyprus readers. Standard Istanbul Turkish, lively and clear; avoid needless English loanwords when a Turkish word is natural (but "kripto para", "blockchain" are fine).
- Numbers: dot for thousands, comma for decimals (1.250.000; 3,5).
- Money: TL figures after the 2005 redenomination need a note ("eski 1 milyon lira = 1 yeni lira").
- Use the circumflex where standard (hikâye) and correct Turkish characters (ı, İ, ğ, ş, ç, ö, ü).

## Reel voice-over and on-screen text — a different craft from captions

These are not shorter captions; they're a different register, and literal translation breaks them worse than it breaks prose.

- **Voiceover** is *spoken*, not written. Draft it by saying the line out loud in your head first, the way a native creator would narrate over a phone video — natural spoken rhythm, contraction-equivalent shortcuts, no written-register connectors you'd never actually say. If it reads like a sentence from an article, rewrite it as something a person would say.
- **On-screen text** (max 6 words) is the hardest spot for translation tells to hide, because compressing a translated sentence into 6 words usually produces something grammatically squeezed that no native speaker would actually type on a video. Don't translate the English on-screen text and then trim it — write the shortest natural phrase a Farsi or Turkish creator would actually caption that beat with, starting from the fact, not from the English words.
- Keep the voiceover and on-screen text for the same beat consistent with each other (they're read/heard at the same moment) but they don't need to be grammatically parallel the way a literal translation would force them to be.
- Timing still matters (FA and TR run a little longer than EN at the same word count — see `post-writer`'s reel spec) but never sacrifice natural phrasing to hit a word count; cut a beat instead.

## Self-check

Compare each FA and TR version against the EN version claim by claim; list any number or date and confirm it is identical.

Then read **every** block aloud in the target language — not just the hook: every caption, every carousel slide, every voiceover line at its stated timing, every on-screen text as if it flashed on a phone screen for two seconds. For each one, ask: would a native creator actually say or write this unscripted, or does it sound like something translated? Rewrite anything that fails — don't just polish it, apply "Write native, don't translate" above and redo that line from the facts.
